# Living guidelines on the responsible use of generative AI in research (ERA Forum Stakeholders' document, fourth version)

Publisher: European Commission, Directorate-General for Research and Innovation (developed within the ERA Forum)
Date: October 2026; the fourth version is stated as published on 15 October 2026 (PDF p. 18); next revision foreseen in Q3 2027
Original: [EU-Kommission-GD-RTD_2026-10-15_era-living-guidelines-genai.pdf](../EU-Kommission-GD-RTD_2026-10-15_era-living-guidelines-genai.pdf)
Bindingness: Empfehlung (non-binding guidance)

## Core statement
A 24-page non-binding guidance document with recommendations for researchers, research organisations and research funding organisations on generative AI, extended in this version to agentic AI. Four principles frame it: consider whether AI is the right tool, accountability stays with researchers and their institutions, confidentiality is non-negotiable, and transparency and disclosure build trust. It translates existing research integrity expectations and states that it creates no new standards (PDF p. 6).

## Relevance to the Leitfrage
### Client projects
- Third parties may use generative AI in meetings, note-taking or summaries of confidential documents. Researchers and organisations should inform third parties about such tools and risks, and tell them when they themselves use AI tools and how collected information is protected (PDF p. 10).
- Researchers should not provide third parties' personal data to external generative AI systems unless a lawful basis such as consent exists and a clear purpose is defined; a footnote adds that bases other than consent are possible (PDF p. 8).
- "The use of AI agents may never be considered a delegation of control or responsibility" (PDF p. 7).
- Candidate rule: tell clients which AI tools and agents touch their material, and do not put client personal data into external systems without a lawful basis and a defined purpose.

### Research
- Responsibility: researchers are accountable for the integrity of AI-supported content. AI systems are neither authors, co-authors nor owners (PDF p. 7).
- Agents: when tasks are implemented autonomously, researchers define check points so that critical decisions, for example methodological ones, are validated by them (PDF p. 7). "AI agents must be deployed strictly as advanced research tools, not independent scientists." Hypotheses, processed data and conclusions of an agent need human validation (PDF p. 14).
- Disclosure: tools used substantially are disclosed with what they contributed, following the rules of discipline, journal, funder or institution. Examples of substantial use are using AI as a method or source, interpreting data analysis, literature review, identifying research gaps, formulating aims, developing hypotheses; basic editorial support is not substantial (PDF p. 7, footnote 10). Spell checking, grammar and formatting generally need no disclosure; "in case of doubt, disclosure is advised" (PDF p. 10).
- Oversight and verification strategies, such as measures against hallucination and bias, should be described so readers can assess validity (PDF p. 7).
- Reproducibility: because generative and agentic AI is non-deterministic, authors decide whether prompts, logs, model versions and parameters should be retained or shared, especially when agents perform sequences of research tasks or contribute to methodological decisions (PDF p. 7).
- Confidentiality: do not upload unpublished or sensitive work into external AI systems unless there are assurances it will not be re-used, for example for training (PDF pp. 7 to 8). Check institutional guidelines, privacy options, who owns the tool and where it runs (PDF p. 8).
- Agents with access to sensitive information: after a suitability assessment the researcher instructs and constrains potential confidentiality breaches and remains accountable for the agent's use (PDF p. 8). Researchers must ensure agents do not breach legislation and are not exempt from legal responsibility because an agent acted on their behalf (PDF p. 8).
- Plagiarism and citations: outputs may rely on others' results and need recognition; the researcher keeps final responsibility for citation correctness (PDF p. 8). Invented citations and wrong summaries even of existing papers: researchers check all references and the content of citations and summaries (PDF p. 9).
- Peer review and proposal evaluation: comply with the applicable rules, never delegate the judgement, use AI only for supporting tasks, do not disclose unpublished proposals, manuscripts or ideas through non-secured tools, prefer tools provided by the institution, funder or publisher, and disclose substantive use (PDF p. 9). AI cannot be used to draft evaluation reports or assess scientific merit (PDF p. 9, footnote 16).
- Applicants: funders are advised to ask applicants to declare substantial AI use and the role of AI in the proposed research (PDF p. 13).
- Candidate rules: a disclosure statement per output type; retain prompts, logs and model versions for agent runs that influence methodology; every agent-produced citation verified against the source.

### Programming
- Coding agents are given as an example of agentic AI (PDF p. 5). Agentic systems are characterised by goal complexity, environmental complexity, adaptability and independent execution, and typically use generative models as reasoning core in a "harness = model + infrastructure" architecture (PDF p. 14).
- The source gives no technical rules for code. Relevant by extension are the points on check points, logging of agent runs, plagiarism in code outputs (PDF p. 8) and prevention of legal breaches by agents.
- A footnote mentions sustainable coding as part of reducing the environmental impact of AI use (PDF p. 8, footnote 15).

### Organisation
- Organisations should train staff in verifying output, privacy, bias and intellectual property, support compliance, and foster an atmosphere where disclosure carries no disadvantage (PDF p. 11).
- Keep track of what generative AI is used for within the organisation (PDF p. 11).
- Whenever possible and necessary, implement self-governed locally hosted or cloud-based tools, or tools governed by trustworthy third parties meeting EU data protection and research security requirements, and ensure appropriate cybersecurity, especially for internet-connected systems (PDF pp. 11 to 12).
- Funders and organisations should account for hidden prompts, meaning invisible instructions embedded in received documents, and set rules and safeguards in IT systems (PDF p. 13). This is stated for funders processing applications, but the risk applies to any agent that reads third-party documents.
- Human oversight models are explained as human in the loop, on the loop and in command (PDF p. 19, footnote 29).
- Candidate rule: a register of approved AI tools with hosting location and data re-use terms, mandatory training, and treatment of third-party documents as possible carriers of hidden instructions.

## Limits
- Non-binding; legislation and funder or institutional requirements prevail (PDF p. 6).
- Addressed to researchers, research organisations and funders; it does not address commercial service providers such as DHCraft, and gives no technical detail on agent security.
- Disclosure thresholds are explicitly described as an open question; a global reporting standard is being developed at the World Conference on Research Integrity 2026 (PDF pp. 14 to 15).
- Footnote 18 notes that disclosure of AI assistance may lead to lower assessment scores in studies (PDF p. 11); the source reports this as a concern, not as a rule.
- Living document; the next revision is foreseen for Q3 2027 (PDF p. 18).
